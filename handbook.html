Welcome

Dear Delegate,

This handbook was made for you — to guide you, answer your questions, and support your preparation from the very first step. Take your time with it, keep it close while you prepare, and treat it as a companion you return to throughout the conference. Every section exists to help you understand the process, take part with confidence, and get the most out of your committee.

A Model UN is a rare kind of event: for three days, the decisions are yours to make and the outcome is yours to shape — much like life itself. So don't be afraid to take risks, step outside your comfort zone, and try the things you have always hesitated over. Growth, learning, and discovery tend to happen exactly where the familiar ends.

And here is the single most useful thing we can tell a new delegate: a Best Delegate is a very busy delegate. The delegates who win are almost never the loudest or the cleverest — they are the ones who simply do more. They speak, they question, they send chits, they lobby, they write. Do enough, and do it well, and you make it statistically impossible to lose. This whole handbook is built around that one idea.

We hope you finish these pages feeling prepared, inspired, and supported. Above all, enjoy it, learn from the people around you, and make the most of this experience. We cannot wait to see you in committee.

Wishing you the very best,

The GTMUN’26 Secretariat

What is MUN?

Model UN is a role-play where you become a country's diplomat and argue your way to a solution.

The United Nations is where the world's countries meet to talk through shared problems — war, climate, trade, human rights — and try to agree on what to do about them. A Model UN simulates that. You are handed a country to represent (your “portfolio” or “allocation”), you research how that country really sees the issue, and then you debate it exactly as that country's diplomat would — even if you personally disagree with them. That last point is the heart of the game: in committee you argue your country's interests, not your own opinions.

A committee is simply a room full of delegates, each speaking for a different country, all working on the same agenda (the topic). Over a few days you will make speeches, question one another, build alliances, and write formal documents — moving, step by step, toward a written solution the room can agree on. Running the room is a small panel called the Executive Board, or “EB” — your chairs. They keep order, guide the debate, answer questions, and score how well each delegate does.

So what do you actually do?

Plenty — and that is the point. In a single committee you will:

  • make speeches setting out your country's position and your ideas;
  • ask and answer questions (“Points of Information”) to test other delegates' arguments;
  • send chits — short written notes — to the EB and to other delegates;
  • lobby — negotiate with delegates who share your goals to build a team;
  • write documents — turning your ideas into formal, votable proposals.

Notice that every one of those is a chance to be marked. We will come back to that idea constantly.

Why bother?

Because few things teach so much at once: speaking with confidence, thinking on your feet, negotiating with people who disagree with you, and writing clearly under pressure. MUN delegates tend to become the people who are comfortable in any room, can hold an argument, and can bring others around to an idea. It is also good fun, you will meet sharp people from across the country, and you will surprise yourself with what you can do by day three.

Remember above all else

MUN is not about “winning the argument.” It is about turning your ideas into a solution the whole room can put its name to. The delegate who solves the problem beats the delegate who only describes it — every single time.

The One Secret: Be a Busy Delegate

Do so much, and do it well, that it becomes statistically impossible for you to lose.

If you remember nothing else from this handbook, remember this. New delegates assume the award goes to the most brilliant speaker. It does not. It goes to the delegate who is present everywhere — who speaks, questions, writes, and negotiates again and again, all weekend, all to a high standard. There is no single moment that wins a MUN. There is a stack of small, good contributions that, added up, leave the Executive Board with no one else to choose.

Here is why this works in your favour. You are scored across many different areas, and they add up. A delegate who gives three good speeches but never writes a document, sends a chit, or lobbies has left most of their marks on the table. A delegate who shows up in every area — even imperfectly — collects from every pile. Effort compounds.

So the strategy is simple: contribute constantly, and make each contribution count. Don't wait for the perfect moment to speak — speak, then improve. Don't write one document — write several. Don't ask one question — ask whenever you spot a flaw. Quantity and quality together are unbeatable; this handbook will teach you both. There is a scorecard near the end to help you track it.

Your First Committee

The hardest part of any MUN is the cold start — walking into a room of strangers before anyone has spoken. So let's remove the mystery. Here is exactly what that first hour looks like, and exactly what to do with it.

What the room looks like

At the front sits the Executive Board (the dais) — your chairs. Facing them are the delegates, each with a placard showing their country's name. You will be given your seat. The session opens with a roll call: the EB reads out each country, and you answer “Present” or “Present and Voting” when yours is called (the difference is explained later — for your first time, “Present” is the obvious choice, since it keeps the option to abstain on a substantive vote if you are unsure). After roll call, debate begins. That's the whole machine; everything else is detail.

Your first hour, step by step

  • Arrive early and settle in. Find your placard, get your notes and laptop out, and take a breath before the room fills. Walking in calm beats walking in late.
  • Introduce yourself to the delegates near you. A simple “Hi, I'm the delegate of France — who are you representing?” is all it takes. You have just started building your alliances, and it costs nothing.
  • Raise your placard early. When the EB opens the General Speakers' List, put your placard up to be added. Speaking once in the first hour — even a short, simple contribution — settles your nerves for the rest of the conference.
  • Listen like it's your job, because it is. As others speak, quietly note three things: who agrees with you (future allies), who opposes you (delegates to win over or rebut), and any factual or logical gaps you can question later. You are already lobbying and preparing your next move.
  • Get a contribution on the board fast. Speak, ask a question, or send a chit within the first hour. The first contribution is the hardest; after it, you are simply a delegate doing the job.
Nervous? That's exactly right

Every good delegate in that room is a little nervous too — nerves are just energy with nowhere to go. Give them somewhere to go by acting early. You do not need a perfect speech or a clever opening line; you need to take part. Confidence is not what you bring to your first contribution. It is what you get from it.

The EB speaks in a particular committee shorthand. Miss the cue and you miss your moment, so here is what the most common phrases actually mean — and what you should do when you hear them.

Speaking in Committee

Speeches and questions are where most of your marks live, and where most beginners freeze. This section gives you the habits, the words, and the safety nets — so that when the placard goes up, you always know what to do.

Speak Like a Diplomat

MUN has its own way of speaking. Get these few habits right and you will instantly sound like you belong — even on day one.

  • Third person, always. Never say “I” or “me.” Say “the delegate of Brazil” or “Brazil believes.” You are a representative, not a private person — this is the rule beginners forget most.
  • Address the dais. Open with “Honourable Chair” or “Thank you, Chair,” and speak to the whole committee, not at a single rival.
  • Attack ideas, never people. “Brazil respectfully disagrees with that approach, because”¦” — never “the delegate is wrong,” and never anything personal. Courtesy is marked; rudeness is penalised.
  • Stay in character. Defend your country's real position, even one you personally dislike. That is the role-play, and arguing off your country's policy will cost you marks.
  • End with an action. Don't just describe the problem — say what the committee should do about it. The delegate who proposes is remembered; the one who only narrates is not.

Saying It Out Loud — Motions & Points

A motion or point is just a fixed sentence you say while standing. Learn the shape once and you can raise anything. The two you will use most:

The moderated caucus (a focused mini-debate)

“The delegate of [Country] moves for a moderated caucus, total time ten minutes, speaking time sixty seconds, on the topic of financing the proposed fund.”

The pattern never changes: total time, speaking time, topic. Say it, sit down, and the committee votes.

The unmoderated caucus (free time to lobby and write)

“The delegate of [Country] moves for an unmoderated caucus for a duration of ten minutes.”

Just a duration. Use the time to find allies and draft your documents.

Raising a point is even shorter. To question a speaker, raise your placard; when recognised, say “Point of Information,” then ask. To flag a factual error or a rules slip, say “Point of Order” and explain briefly.

How to Answer a Point of Information You Don't Know

Sooner or later a delegate will ask you something you cannot answer. This happens to everyone, and you can handle it without losing face. The three rules: never freeze, never invent a fake fact, never get defensive. Instead, do one of these:

  • Bridge to what you do know. “While [Country] does not have that exact figure to hand, what we can say is”¦” — then pivot straight to your strongest point.
  • Reframe the question. “The more important question for this committee is”¦” — and answer that one instead.
  • Promise to follow up — then actually do it. “[Country] will address that point in a substantive chit to the dais.” Now you've turned a tough question into another scored contribution.
  • Turn it back, sparingly. “[Country] would welcome the delegate's own view on that.” Useful once; overused, it looks evasive.

A Tough Portfolio? You Can Still Shine

Drew a tiny country, or an observer with no vote? Do not deflate — some of the most impressive delegates play small portfolios brilliantly. In committee, influence comes from ideas and relationships, not from the size of your nation.

  • Become the expert. Pick one corner of the agenda and know it better than anyone in the room. A small country with one sharp, specific idea gets listened to.
  • Be the bridge. Small, neutral states make the best mediators between bigger rival blocs. Broker the compromise and your name ends up on every document.
  • Sell your swing. If your vote is genuinely up for grabs, blocs will court you — trade it for your priorities being written into the draft.
  • Observers: write and persuade. You may not vote, but you can speak, lobby, and co-author. A persuasive observer shapes what the voters end up voting on.
The thread running through all of this

Whatever your portfolio, your job is the same: bring solutions, not just opinions. Every speech, question, and chit should move the committee one step closer to fixing the problem.

Chits

A chit is a short written note — the quiet, parallel conversation running underneath every committee, and a steady source of marks.

Debate time is limited, and you will not be recognised to speak as often as you would like. Chits are the answer. They let you keep contributing — making points, asking questions, and negotiating — in writing, even when you do not hold the floor. At GTMUN’26, chits are submitted online; the exact tool and process will be explained by your Executive Board before the conference. Whatever the medium, the three kinds and the etiquette below stay the same.

Keep every chit in clear, formal, diplomatic language, and keep it on the agenda. Irrelevant, abusive, or off-topic chits are discarded. But — in the spirit of the busy delegate — do not be shy about sending them: over a weekend, your chits add up to a real share of your total marks. Send a chit whenever you have a genuine point and no floor to say it on.

The Three Kinds of Chit

1. Substantive chit (to the Executive Board). An argument, analysis, or solution you want credited but could not fit into a speech. This is the one that earns you marks, so make it count: go beyond stating a fact — explain why it matters, how it connects to the agenda, and what the committee should do about it.

2. Point of Information chit (to another delegate, via the EB). A written question to another delegate, routed through the dais. It lets you challenge an argument even when you are not on the floor. The delegate may reply, but is never obliged to.

3. Transitive chit (delegate to delegate). A private note to another delegate for lobbying, clarifying, or coordinating. These are not marked — but stay professional and on-topic even here.

What a Strong Chit Looks Like

A substantive chit, weak versus strong:

WeakNorway has the highest EV adoption in the world. We think everyone should follow our model.
StrongNorway's EV transition only worked because incentives were paired with charging infrastructure — subsidies alone failed in markets without it. This committee's fund should therefore tie any subsidy to a matching infrastructure commitment, or it will repeat that failure.

Why: The strong chit doesn't just state a fact — it explains the mechanism, draws a lesson, and turns it into a concrete recommendation the committee can act on.

A Point of Information chit, weak versus strong:

WeakDoes the delegate of Brazil even have a real plan?
StrongBrazil demands both absolute national sovereignty and binding international inspections. How does the delegate reconcile compelling inspections on a sovereign state with the non-interference principle Brazil just invoked?

Why: The weak version is a personal jab the EB will discard. The strong one exposes a genuine contradiction inside Brazil's own argument — which is exactly what a question is for, and what earns marks.

The golden rule of chits

A substantive chit is a silent speech. Make it argue, not just inform — every chit should leave the dais with a flaw exposed, a link drawn, or a solution proposed. Then send them often: many good chits beat one perfect one you never sent.

Documentation

Documentation is the written half of MUN — and the half where busy delegates quietly pull ahead. It is also the most under-used: many delegates speak well but barely write, leaving a whole pile of marks untouched. Don't be one of them. This section walks you through every document, step by step, with examples of what good actually looks like.

There are four kinds of document at GTMUN’26: Position Papers, Working Papers, Resolutions (and other end documents), and Press Releases. Only the position paper is compulsory; the rest are optional but heavily rewarded — so write them. As a rule, send a few detailed documents rather than many shallow ones; quality is marked, not word-count. Everything is typed and emailed to your committee's Executive Board (you may also handwrite, scan, and email). Nothing is accepted in physical form.

A. Position Paper

What it is. Your one compulsory document, written before the conference. It sets out your country's stance on the agenda and the ideas you hope to push in committee — think of it as walking in with a plan already on paper. A strong position paper tells the EB, before you have said a word, that you are prepared.

What it must contain — three things, in order:

  • What your country has already done about the issue — past actions, treaties signed, policies passed.
  • The core problems your country believes the committee must solve.
  • Your proposed solutions — and, honestly, the challenges each one faces. This is the part the EB reads most closely, so make your solutions concrete.

Format. Around two pages, 12-point font, single-spaced, justified. In the heading put the committee, your country, and the agenda — but never your name or school. Cite any especially important sources in the text.

B. Working Paper

What it is. A collection of the solutions you and others develop during committee — the rough draft of ideas that comes before any formal end document. Working papers are informal: they need not follow a strict format, and they are not voted on. Once refined, they can grow into your committee's final document.

A solution clause, weak versus strong:

WeakWe should raise awareness about the issue and encourage all countries to cooperate for a better future.
StrongEstablish a regional Rapid-Response Fund, seeded by a 0.1% levy on member-state trade, that disburses within thirty days of a verified emergency and is audited annually by an independent panel.

Why: The weak clause is a vague hope no one can act on or argue against. The strong one names the mechanism, the money, the trigger, the timeline, and the oversight — a real, debatable solution the committee can build on.

More is better — one is rarely enough

Don't write a single working paper with one idea and stop. Develop several solutions, each into a real, well-built clause — and send more than one paper if you have more than one strong idea. This is the busy-delegate principle in action: the delegate with five solid solutions on the table is far harder to overlook than the one with a single line.

C. Press Release

What it is. A short, formal statement in which your country announces a position, a policy shift, or a response to another delegate's actions. At MUN these are usually shared only with the EB (and other delegates when released), so use them when a moment genuinely calls for a formal, documented response. Keep the tone diplomatic, factual, and concise.

When to use one. To make an impactful announcement; as a joint statement when several countries share a stance (a show of unity); or to formally register protest or condemnation of an action.

D. Resolution (and other end documents)

What it is. The committee's final product — the formal document that records what the room has decided to do. Your working-paper ideas, once polished and properly formatted, become a draft resolution; whatever the committee votes through becomes the resolution. Learning its anatomy is the single most useful documentation skill you can have, because the same structure underlies almost every end document.

Sponsors and signatories — what they actually mean

Sponsors are the principal authors of the document. They agree with its content, they own it, and they cannot withdraw their sponsorship once it has been formally introduced. Being a sponsor is a strong public signal that you led the work — aim to be one. The maximum number of sponsors is 10% of the committee's strength, up to a cap of five.

Signatories are delegates who simply want the document to be debated. Signing does not mean they support it — only that they think it is worth the committee's time. Collecting signatures is itself a lobbying exercise, and a good way to be busy. A document usually needs signatories equal to at least 20% of the committee's strength before it can be introduced.

The two parts of a resolution

1. Preambulatory clauses set the scene — they describe the problem and recall past efforts (the “why”). They are not voted on individually, and each begins with an italicised participle:

Recognizing, Reaffirming, Noting with concern, Recalling, Deeply disturbed, Bearing in mind, Emphasizing”¦

2. Operative clauses are the action — the “what we will do.” They are numbered, can be amended and voted on, and each begins with an active verb:

Calls upon, Urges, Recommends, Requests, Encourages, Establishes, Decides, Invites, Affirms, Welcomes”¦

(An amendment is just a proposed change to an operative clause — an addition, deletion, or reword. If every sponsor agrees it is “friendly” and slips in automatically; if not, it is “unfriendly” and the committee votes on it. The full rules are in the Annexure.)

An operative clause, weak versus strong:

Weak1. Urges all countries to do more about the problem and work together for a better future;
Strong1. Calls upon member states to establish a shared early-warning network, coordinated by the existing UN office, funded through voluntary contributions and reporting annually to this committee;

Why: The weak clause is a vague wish no one can act on or oppose. The strong clause names who acts, what they do, who runs it, how it is paid for, and how it is tracked — a real, debatable proposal.

Not every committee writes a “resolution”

Most committees do — but several produce a differently-named final document. The skill is the same (preamble-style context, then numbered actions); only the name and tone change. Know what your room is actually building toward:

Marksheets — How You Are Scored

Every committee is judged on the same core areas, though the weight of each may shift with the committee's size and nature. The lesson is the busy-delegate principle in another form: you have to show up in every area. A delegate who gives brilliant speeches but never lobbies or writes a document has simply left most of the marks unclaimed. Spread yourself across all seven, and do each well, and the marks stack up in your favour.

Two things worth holding on to

1. You are never punished for taking part. The Executive Board will not average your submissions, and will not mark you down simply for participating. The more you do, the more you can gain — never less. (The only exceptions are foreign policy and diplomatic courtesy, explained below.)

2. You will see exactly how you did. Full marksheets are shared after the conference, broken down by area, so you can see precisely where your marks came from and where to grow.

The seven areas are speeches, lobbying, diplomatic courtesy, points, substantive chits, POI chits, and documentation. Running through all of them is one quality the Board rewards above any other: solving the problem. A contribution that moves the committee toward a real solution always outscores one that only describes the problem.

1. Speeches

Your speeches are judged on three things, in rising order of importance:

Research — the depth at which you understand the topic. This is not the citing of sources; it is grasping that problems which look simple are in fact deeply nuanced.

Analysis — how you build on that research: how you structure the speech, frame your ideas, and connect the dots. This is the most important part — it is where you make your case. Note that good research does not automatically produce good analysis.

Impact — delivery and effect. We judge not only how you speak, but how your speech lands on the other delegates, and how its ideas carry into your chits, lobbying, and documents.

To score well: prepare your key points but stay flexible; make your country's priorities clear and explain why they matter; and above all propose solutions, not just problems — the Board notices the delegate who offers a way forward. Build on what others have said (“Building on the delegate of X”¦”) to show you are listening.

To avoid: reading a speech word-for-word, going off-topic, repeating what has already been said, and speaking too quietly or too fast.

2. Lobbying

Lobbying is how you build relationships, influence others, and get your ideas into the committee's final document — mostly during unmoderated caucuses and breaks. What counts: building coalitions with delegates who share your goals; persuading and negotiating toward common ground; staying active outside formal debate; and showing flexibility without abandoning your country's core position. Good lobbying is not shouting — it is listening, and being collaborative rather than authoritarian. Identify allies early, help improve their ideas as well as pushing your own, and keep track of the informal agreements you reach.

3. Diplomatic Courtesy

How you conduct yourself: professionalism, respect for others, and positive contribution to debate, in both your words and your demeanour. Pay attention when others speak, follow the Chair's directions, and never interrupt, mock, or dismiss. This area carries negative marking — discourtesy actively costs you. It is the easiest area to keep perfect, and the easiest to throw away.

4. Points

Raising points well shows the Board you are attentive, analytical, and engaged.

Points of Information (your questions). The purpose of a question is not to extract more research from another delegate — it is to find a logical or inherent flaw in their argument and expose it. A good question therefore shows the Board three things at once: that you are attentive and listening, that you are analytical and comprehend the debate, and that you are well read on the agenda. Keep questions concise, relevant, and diplomatic; quality matters far more than quantity.

Points of Order. No marks are awarded for pointing out a procedural error the Board may have made. But if you point out a genuine factual inaccuracy in another delegate's speech and the Board rules in your favour, you are awarded marks in proportion to the significance of the inaccuracy.

5. Substantive Chits

Written contributions to the Executive Board that let you share ideas you could not fit into a speech. What counts: relevance to the agenda; analysis and insight (explain why your point matters, do not just state a fact); clarity and structure; and originality. Make them actionable wherever you can — suggest a solution, a clause, or a recommendation. One strong, well-argued chit beats five weak ones — but, true to the busy-delegate rule, send strong chits often.

6. POI Chits

Written questions to other delegates, routed through the Board. They let you engage even when you do not hold the floor, and show the same attentiveness and analysis as a verbal question. What counts: relevance, clarity, and concision in your questions — and thoughtful, complete replies when you are questioned (you need not reply to every chit you receive, but a good reply demonstrates knowledge and diplomacy). Avoid off-topic questions and one-word answers.

7. Documentation

All the written materials you submit — position papers, working papers, press releases, and the committee's end document. This is where preparation shows. It is evaluated on substance (knowledge and thoughtful analysis), relevance (does it address the agenda and the committee's goals), originality (are the proposals genuinely your own and well-thought-out), and collaboration (for working papers and resolutions, did you work effectively with others). Start early, keep documents neat and well-structured, make them reflect your country's stance, and proofread — and write more than the bare minimum.

Foreign policy — the one place staying in character matters most

Across every area above, you must argue your country's real position. Speaking or writing against your own country's policy carries negative marking. The role-play is the point: a delegate who breaks character has stopped playing the game the Board is scoring.

What the Executive Board is actually looking for

Decode all seven areas into one picture. From the dais, the delegate who stands out is the one who:

Solves, never just describes. Brings actions, clauses, and solutions — in every area.

Is everywhere. Speaks, questions, chits, lobbies, and writes — the busy delegate, collecting from every pile.

Builds on the room. References others and moves the debate forward, rather than just being loud.

Stays in character. Argues their country's real interests, consistently.

Lifts others. Improves other delegates' ideas; the Board notices the person everyone wants to work with.

How to Win MUNs

Marksheets tell you what is judged. This tells you how to actually do it. If you read one section twice, make it this one.

Winning a MUN is not about talking the most or the loudest — it is about preparation, ideas, and the trust you build with the room. And it runs on the busy-delegate engine: do a great deal, across every area, all weekend. Here is the whole arc, in five moves.

1. Prepare

Everyone says “do your research.” But research is only collecting information; preparation is knowing how to use it. Preparation means walking in with a game plan — you know what your country stands for, what you will say, and how you will respond when things get messy. That is what gives you confidence, and in MUN, confidence is everything.

  • Frame the topic. Break the agenda down: who are the key players, what has been tried, which angles matter — security, economy, rights?
  • Build your country's policy. Pick the two or three sub-issues your country cares about most — your “signature points” — and learn them inside out.
  • Plan your moves. Who are your natural allies? Who blocks you? Which solutions will you push, and how — speech, caucus, or document?
  • Rehearse. Say your speeches out loud and picture the stages of committee. If you have “seen” yourself do it, you will be calmer in the moment.

2. Inspire

Anyone can make a speech, and most delegates do — they stand, state their country's view, list a few solutions, and then the room forgets it. Routine speeches are just noise. If you want to lead, your words have to spark something: make delegates lean in, make the Chair remember your name. Because once you inspire, you influence — and once you influence, you lead.

Carry one to three ideas per speech. You usually get only a minute or two — enough for a small handful of clear, powerful points, not a lecture. Choose your best one to three, and drive them home. And never just state a fact; say what the committee should do about it.

A speech argument, weak versus strong:

WeakIndia is a developing country with a large population and many challenges. We care deeply about this issue and have always supported international cooperation. We believe everyone should work together.
StrongIndia will not sign a fund that exists only on paper. We propose three things this committee can do today: a fixed contribution formula so no nation can free-ride, an independent body to audit where every rupee goes, and a first disbursement within ninety days. India will sponsor any draft that carries all three — and oppose any that carries none.

Why: The weak version recites the country and proposes nothing. The strong one takes a clear position, names three concrete actions, ties them to how India will vote, and gives the room something to rally around.

Then deliver it. Slow down. Project from your chest, not by yelling. Stand tall, make eye contact, and use your hands with purpose. People see you before they hear you — walk with intent.

3. Connect

Lobbying can feel like chaos — people darting around pitching half-baked ideas. But leaders do not just throw out ideas; they connect people, ideas, and resources into a bloc that actually works. Before the first caucus, use your soft power: you have said hi and passed a few notes — now find out who genuinely wants to work with you. See delegates as teammates: who has trust to share, who has strong ideas, who has the research and writing power?

Build rapport, then read the room. People follow people they like — use phrases like “that is a good point, and I would add”¦”, ask about them as people, and above all listen more than you talk. Notice when the room is ready for a new idea and when it just wants to be heard. Leadership is not talking loudest; it is people talking to you because they trust you.

4. Negotiate

Many delegates think passing a document is about winning the debate. It is not — the people you are arguing with will not vote for you anyway. If your goal is to get your draft passed, you negotiate, not debate. Negotiation is knowing what you want, what you cannot give away, and how to bring others on board.

  • Count your votes. Start with friends and like-minded delegates; add sponsors wherever you can — a sponsor is almost always a guaranteed vote.
  • Handle objections. Often it is the wording, not the idea — reframe it. Don't waste time on delegates who only want to argue.
  • Drive mergers. If documents merge, lead the process and keep your core ideas intact. Negotiate on interests, not ego.

5. Brand

A Best Delegate wins and leaves a legacy. Medals are short-term; your brand — how people remember you — lasts your whole MUN career. Branding is reputation plus relationships: perform well, but also act with diplomacy, respect, and collaboration, so delegates want to work with you again. Reflect after every conference (what worked, what didn't), network beyond the sessions, and remember the mission is bigger than awards — MUN helps you find your passions, grow as a leader, and prepare to change the world.

The trap to avoid

Don't try to win by dominating. The delegate who interrupts, recites endless facts, or bulldozes others reads as insecure, not strong. Influence is quiet: be the one whose ideas everyone ends up building on — and the one who simply, visibly, did more than anyone else.

Researching Your Country

You do not need a week — about thirty focused minutes gets you genuinely prepared. Here is exactly where to go and what to look for.

Where to go

  • Your country's Ministry of Foreign Affairs website — its official positions and recent statements, straight from the source.
  • The UN's own records — un.org, and the UN Digital Library (digitallibrary.un.org) to see how your country actually voted and what it said on this kind of issue.
  • Reliable news — Reuters, AP, BBC, or Al Jazeera for current, real-world context.
  • The CIA World Factbook (cia.gov/the-world-factbook) — for quick, reliable basics on any country.
  • The parent body's website — e.g., the WTO, INTERPOL, or UNEP site for how that organisation works and what it can do.

What to find — four questions

  • What does my country want? Its core interest on this agenda.
  • What does it fear? The outcome it most wants to avoid.
  • Who are its allies and rivals? Where your bloc will come from.
  • What has it already done? A treaty, law, or action you can cite — this is what makes you sound real.

How to read a background guide — fast

Your committee's background guide can look long. Don't read it like a novel; mine it. In one pass, find and mark these five things, and you have everything you need:

  • The agenda statement — what, in one line, the committee is actually being asked to solve.
  • The players — who the major countries and groups are, and where yours sits among them.
  • The fault lines — the two or three core disagreements the committee keeps returning to. This is where debate will live.
  • Your country's angle — wherever your country (or your bloc) is mentioned, and how it lines up on each fault line.
  • The questions — most guides end with discussion questions. They are, in effect, a preview of what you will debate — answer them for yourself in advance.
Let the prep worksheet do the work

Each committee has a Delegate Prep Worksheet that walks you through all of this, agenda by agenda. Fill it in once and your speeches, chits, and position paper are already half-written.

Glossary

Every term you will hear in committee, in one place. Skim it once now; the rest will click into place in context.

Abstain — To formally not vote either way on a substantive matter (only “Present” delegates may).

Agenda — The topic the committee is debating.

Amendment — A proposed change to an operative clause — an addition, deletion, or reword.

Bloc — A group of delegates with shared interests who work together.

Caucus — A pause in formal debate — moderated (focused debate on a sub-topic) or unmoderated (free movement and lobbying).

Dais — The Executive Board's table; the people running the committee.

Delegate — You — the representative of a country or portfolio.

Draft Resolution — A formal written proposal, debated and voted on; the committee's main product.

Executive Board (EB) — Your chairs — they run the room and score you.

Foreign Policy — Your country's real positions; staying true to them is marked (and breaking from them is penalised).

GSL — General Speakers' List — the default queue of speakers.

Lobbying — Negotiating with other delegates to build support, usually during unmoderated caucus.

Motion — A formal proposal to do something procedural (e.g., open a caucus).

Observer — A delegate who can speak and lobby but cannot vote on substantive matters.

Operative clause — A numbered, action clause in a resolution — the “what we will do.”

Placard — Your country's name card; raise it to be recognised.

Point of Information (POI) — A question to a delegate who has the floor.

Point of Order (POO) — Flags a procedural error or a factual inaccuracy.

Portfolio — The country or character you represent.

Preambulatory clause — An opening clause in a resolution that sets the scene — the “why.”

Quorum — The minimum attendance needed for debate (one-third of members).

Resolution — An adopted draft resolution — the committee's final decision.

Signatory — A delegate who wants a document debated (not necessarily a supporter).

Sponsor — A principal author of a document, who agrees with its content.

Substantive vs Procedural — Substantive = the content (resolutions, amendments); procedural = how the committee runs.

Yield — To give up your remaining speaking time — to the Chair, another delegate, or questions.

Annexure — GTMUN Rules of Procedure

Authored: Vedansh Arora, MUN Advisor

Read this later, not now

This section is technical, and you do not need to read it before your first committee. Your Executive Board will explain the procedure to you in due time, and you will pick most of it up simply by taking part.

It lives here, at the back, as a reference for when you need it — above all, when there is a disagreement about the rules. This is the document that settles such disputes. Keep it on hand; reach for it only when a question of procedure actually arises.

Section 1: General Rules

Rule 1. Official Language. The official language of debate in all committees is English. Delegates must draft all speeches, working papers, draft resolutions, and amendments in English only.

Rule 2. Quorum.

  • 2.1 A quorum is required for a committee to begin its proceedings. A quorum is defined as the presence of at least one-third of the total member states in the committee. If quorum is lost during the session, the Chair will suspend debate until quorum is re-established.
  • 2.2 Quorum is necessary for debate to start, but a simple majority (more than half of the present members) is required for any substantive vote (such as voting on draft resolutions).

Rule 3. Decorum.

  • 3.1 Delegates must maintain formal decorum at all times. This includes appropriate professional attire (Western formals).
  • 3.2 Delegates must refrain from offensive language, personal attacks, or disruption of debate.
  • 3.3 All placards, laptops, or devices should be used only for committee-related purposes. Chit-passing is allowed only through the Secretariat or as permitted by the Chair.
  • 3.4 Any delegate found violating decorum may receive warnings from the Chair. Repeated or serious violations can result in expulsion from the committee.

Rule 4. Authority of the Chair.

  • 4.1 The Chair is the highest authority in the committee. Their decisions are final and binding on all delegates. The Chair is responsible for maintaining order, ensuring fair debate, and enforcing rules of procedure.
  • 4.2 If a delegate feels that the Chair has made an error, they may raise an Appeal to the Decision. Any appeals will be addressed by the Head of MUN, or where applicable, the Secretary-General and the MUN Advisor.

Section 2: Agenda & Debate Structure

Rule 5. Roll Call.

  • 5.1 At the start of each session, the Chair shall conduct a roll call to establish attendance and determine quorum.
  • 5.2 When called, each delegate must respond with: “Present and Voting”, the delegate is in attendance and will vote on all procedural and substantive matters; “Present”, the delegate is in attendance, will vote on all procedural matters, but may abstain on substantive votes.
  • 5.3 Delegates who fail to respond during roll call will be marked absent. They may be added to the roll later by informing the Chair.
  • 5.4 Procedural Voting concerns the functioning of the committee (e.g., setting the agenda, motions, suspension/adjournment of the meeting, etc.). All delegates must vote on procedural matters (no abstentions are allowed).
  • 5.5 Substantive Voting concerns the actual content of draft resolutions and amendments. Only Member States may vote (Observers do not vote on substantive matters).

Rule 6. Setting the Agenda.

  • 6.1 At the beginning of the first session, the committee must determine which agenda item will be taken up first. In cases where this is the only agenda, the agenda is set by default.
  • 6.2 Any delegate may motion to set the agenda to one of the allotted topics. If multiple motions are raised, they shall be voted upon in the order in which they were introduced. A simple majority (more votes in favor than against) is required to adopt an agenda. Abstentions are permitted in this vote.
  • 6.3 Once adopted, debate shall proceed only on that agenda item until it is formally closed. Closed agenda items may not be reopened.

Rule 7. General Speakers”™ List (GSL).

  • 7.1 The GSL is the default form of debate. At the start of the debate, when the Chair opens the GSL, delegates wishing to be added must raise their placards to be recognised.
  • 7.2 The speaking time for the GSL is set by the committee at the start of the debate (typically 60–180 seconds) and communicated to the chair through a motion. The default time for the speech is 90 seconds.
  • 7.3 Delegates may yield their remaining time in only three ways:
  • To the Chair (remaining time is forfeited).
  • To another delegate — the time will not be added as an extension to their upcoming speech; it is an extension to be used at that moment, if they accept.
  • To questions (questions related to the speech). A delegate seeking to follow up on an answer may raise a plea to the Chair, which the Chair may grant at the Executive Board”™s discretion where the question has not been sufficiently answered.

Rule 8. Moderated Caucus.

  • 8.1 A moderated caucus is a focused debate on a specific sub-topic of the agenda, conducted under the Chair”™s moderation. To motion for one, a delegate must specify the total duration, the speaking time per delegate, and the specific sub-topic (which must relate to the current agenda).
  • 8.2 The motion is put to an immediate procedural vote and requires a simple majority. A moderated caucus may be extended only once, by no more than its original duration.
  • 8.3 The Chair selects speakers at their discretion to maintain balance; the delegate who raised the motion may choose to speak first or to be recognised in the regular order of speakers.
  • 8.4 Points of Information and Points of Order may be permitted during a moderated caucus, subject to a procedural vote of the committee.

Rule 9. Unmoderated Caucus.

  • 9.1 An unmoderated caucus suspends formal debate to allow free discussion, lobbying, and drafting of documents.
  • 9.2 To motion for an unmoderated caucus, a delegate must specify the duration (e.g., 10 minutes). A simple majority is required to pass the motion. Abstentions are not allowed.
  • 9.3 There is no limit to the maximum total time.

Rule 10. Open House Discussion.

  • 10.1 An Open House Discussion is a semi-formal format of debate that allows for a free flow of ideas without the restriction of fixed speaking times per delegate.
  • 10.2 To motion for an Open House Discussion, a delegate must specify the total duration and the topic of discussion.
  • 10.3 Once the motion is passed, the Chair will open the floor and recognise delegates in the order that placards are raised. Unlike a moderated caucus, no individual speaking time is set; delegates may speak until they have completed their intervention, at which point the Chair immediately recognizes another placard. The Chair has the authority to cut off speeches that are excessively long, repetitive, or off-topic to maintain fairness. Delegates may not yield time in this format.
  • 10.4 A motion for an Open House Discussion is a procedural motion and requires a simple majority to pass. Abstentions are not permitted.

Rule 11. Closure of Debate. Any delegate may motion to close debate when the committee has sufficiently discussed the agenda item. A simple majority is required to pass; abstentions are not permitted. If passed, the debate immediately ends, and the committee proceeds to voting on draft resolutions.

Section 3: Points & Motions

Rule 12. General Rules on Points and Motions. Points and motions are the procedural tools through which delegates may request actions from the Chair or clarify matters of debate. All points and motions must be raised only when the floor is open, unless otherwise specified. Delegates must raise their placards and wait to be recognised by the Chair before speaking.

Rule 13. Point of Personal Privilege.

  • 13.1 A delegate may raise this point when a personal condition is preventing them from participating effectively in debate (for example: inability to hear, temperature of the room, or discomfort). The Chair shall immediately attempt to resolve the concern.
  • 13.2 This point may interrupt a speaker only if the issue is related to audibility (e.g., “Can the speaker be requested to speak louder?”).
  • 13.3 A Point of Personal Privilege may also be raised immediately after a speech has concluded to request the repetition of the speech if the delegate could not hear or understand it.

Rule 14. Point of Order.

  • 14.1 A delegate may raise this point to correct a procedural error made during the course of debate (e.g., the chair forgot to take more speakers when time was remaining, etc). It may not interrupt a speaker. The Chair will rule on the point immediately. This point cannot be used to challenge the Chair”™s substantive judgment (e.g., choice of speakers, recognition order).
  • 14.2 A delegate may also raise this point when another delegate makes a statement containing an objective factual inaccuracy (e.g., misquoting a treaty date). This may not interrupt a speaker. A point of order should be submitted verbally, or in cases where it is not possible, via a chit. A chair may choose only to accept POOs in writing.
  • 14.3 The delegate raising the point must state the correction clearly. If necessary, the Chair may request supporting documentation (e.g., official UN sources). This point cannot be used to express disagreement with an opinion or interpretation.

Rule 15. Point of Inquiry. A delegate may raise this point to enquire of the Chair regarding the rules of procedure. It may not interrupt a speaker. Delegates should not use this point to enquire about substantive issues (policy, etc.).

Rule 16. Point of Information.

  • 16.1 A Point of Information is a question directed to a delegate who has the floor. The delegate asking a PoI must be recognised by the Chair. They must pose the question concisely and without making a statement or comment.
  • 16.2 Follow-up PoIs (“PoI to the PoI”) are not permitted unless explicitly allowed by the Chair.
  • 16.3 If multiple delegates raise their placards for PoIs, the Chair will select who may ask and how many questions are permitted.

Rule 17. Right of Reply.

  • 17.1 A delegate may request a Right of Reply if they feel that their personal dignity as a representative has been insulted by another delegate”™s speech.
  • 17.2 The request must be submitted in writing to the Chair, clearly identifying the offending statement. The Chair will decide whether to grant the Right of Reply. If granted, the delegate will be allowed to respond briefly (the time limit will be set by the Chair).

Rule 18. Motions.

  • 18.1 Motions are used to move the committee into different types of debate or to take specific actions. All motions must be phrased formally, e.g., “The delegate of [Country] moves to”¦”
  • 18.2 When several motions are raised, the following order of disruption applies (from highest to lowest priority):
  • Motion to Suspend the Meeting (for breaks, meals, etc.).
  • Motion for an Unmoderated Caucus (informal discussion without the Chair”™s moderation).
  • Motion for a Moderated Caucus (formal discussion with shorter, fixed speaking times).
  • Motion to Change the Speaking Time (adjusting duration of speeches).
  • Motion to Close Debate (end debate on the topic and move to voting).
  • Motion to Re-Order Draft Resolutions (change the order in which they are voted on).
  • Motion to Adjourn the Meeting (end the session permanently).

Section 4: End Documentation

Rule 19. Resolutions.

  • 19.1 A draft resolution is a written proposal that contains the committee”™s recommendations on the agenda under discussion.
  • 19.2 Any delegate may submit a draft resolution, provided it has the required number of sponsors and signatories.
  • Sponsors are the principal authors of a draft resolution. They must agree to the content and may not withdraw their sponsorship once the draft has been formally introduced. The maximum number of sponsors is 10% of the committee strength up to 5 sponsors.
  • Signatories are delegates who wish for the draft resolution to be discussed. Signing does not imply support of the content; it only indicates willingness to debate. The minimum number of signatories required for a resolution is 20% of the committee strength.
  • 19.3 Draft resolutions must be submitted in writing to the Chair, who will review them for formatting and relevance before allowing their introduction.

Rule 20. Introduction of Draft Resolutions. A draft resolution may be introduced once the Chair has approved it and the committee has reached the appropriate stage of debate. The introduction of a Resolution requires a simple majority vote. The Chair will announce the draft resolution”™s sponsors and signatories and assign it a number (e.g., “Draft Resolution 1.1”).

Rule 21. Discussion on Resolutions. Once a Draft Resolution has been introduced, the committee may discuss it through a moderated caucus dedicated to the DR (as a whole or a particular section, raisable on multiple resolutions at once); clause-by-clause debate (at the Chair”™s discretion or by motion, with operative clauses taken up individually and questions raised to the sponsors, on one resolution at a time); or a Provisional Speakers List (a shorter list dedicated exclusively to the DR, on which each delegate delivers a structured statement of their delegation”™s stance).

Rule 22. Amendments.

  • 22.1 An amendment is a change proposed to an existing draft resolution. Friendly Amendments: if all sponsors of a draft resolution agree to the change, it is automatically incorporated without a vote. Unfriendly Amendments: if one or more sponsors do not agree, the amendment must be put to a substantive vote by the committee.
  • 22.2 Amendments must be submitted in writing to the Chair before being discussed or voted upon. Delegates may propose an Addition (inserting a new clause, sub-clause, or phrase), a Deletion (removing an existing clause, sub-clause, or phrase), or a Modification (altering the wording of an existing clause without deleting it entirely).

Rule 23. Voting on Resolutions and Amendments. All votes on draft resolutions, amendments, and division of the question are substantive in nature. Only Member States may vote; Observers may not. Each Member State has one vote. A substantive matter passes with a simple majority (more votes in favour than against), unless otherwise specified by the committee”™s mandate. Abstentions are not counted towards the total. Voting shall normally be by show of placards. A roll-call vote may be requested by any delegate and granted at the Chair”™s discretion.

Section 5: Miscellaneous Rules

Rule 24. Suspension and Adjournment of the Committee.

  • 24.1 A motion to suspend the committee may be raised if delegates wish to take a break (for meals, rest, or logistical reasons). This motion requires a simple majority to pass.
  • 24.2 A motion to adjourn the committee is raised when the body wishes to conclude its work for the day or permanently end the session.

Rule 25. Attendance and Participation.

  • 25.1 Delegates are expected to be present throughout committee sessions. Absences should be informed to the Chair and the Secretariat in advance, wherever possible.
  • 25.2 A delegate who is not present during roll call shall be marked absent. Upon late arrival, they may request the dais to update their status to “Present” or “Present and Voting.”

Rule 26. Conduct and Decorum.

  • 26.1 Delegates must uphold the highest standards of courtesy, professionalism, and respect toward fellow delegates, the dais, and staff members.
  • 26.2 The use of offensive, discriminatory, or derogatory language is strictly forbidden. Any personal attack, whether verbal or written, will result in immediate warning and possible expulsion from the committee.
  • 26.3 Delegates must refrain from disrupting debate through side conversations, excessive noise, or inappropriate behaviour. The Chair has full authority to call any delegate to order.

Rule 27. Precedence of Rules. These rules of procedure shall apply to all committee sessions unless otherwise modified by the Secretariat before the commencement of the conference. In case of conflict between these rules and the Charter of the United Nations or the official mandate of the committee being simulated, the latter shall prevail.

Rule 28. Amendments to Rules.

  • 28.1 Any change, suspension, or exception to these rules must be approved by a simple majority vote of the committee, with the final discretion of the Chair.
  • 28.2 The Secretariat reserves the right to overrule any such change if it conflicts with the overall framework of the conference.

Rule 29. Use of Electronic Devices & Internet.

  • 29.1 Delegates are permitted to use laptops, tablets, and other electronic devices during committee sessions for research, drafting, and communication purposes, provided this does not disturb the debate.
  • 29.2 Internet access is permitted for all participants.
  • 29.3 Any misuse of devices (e.g., distracting activities, irrelevant browsing, or inappropriate communication) may result in the withdrawal of this privilege by the Chair.

Rule 30. Spirit of the Rules. The purpose of these rules is to ensure orderly, fair, and constructive debate, enabling delegates to work together toward diplomatic solutions. Delegates are reminded that procedure is a tool for cooperation, not a barrier to it.
